Understanding Chronic Kidney Disease

Your kidneys are remarkable organs. Learn what happens when they need help — and what you can do about it.

🫘 What Do Your Kidneys Do?

Your two kidneys — each about the size of your fist — work around the clock to keep your body in balance. Think of them as your body's filtering system.

  • Filter waste — removing toxins, excess fluid, and metabolic byproducts from your blood
  • Balance electrolytes — keeping sodium, potassium, calcium, and phosphorus at safe levels
  • Regulate blood pressure — producing renin, a hormone that helps control blood pressure
  • Stimulate red blood cells — producing erythropoietin (EPO) to prevent anemia
  • Activate vitamin D — helping your bones absorb calcium and stay strong

Every day, your kidneys filter about 150 litres of blood and produce 1–2 litres of urine. When they lose function, these critical jobs go undone — that's CKD.

📋 What is Chronic Kidney Disease?

CKD means your kidneys have been damaged and can't filter blood as well as they should. "Chronic" means the damage has been present for more than 3 months and is usually progressive.

Common Causes

  • Diabetes (Type 1 or Type 2) — the #1 cause worldwide
  • High blood pressure (hypertension) — the #2 cause
  • Glomerulonephritis — inflammation of the kidney's filtering units
  • Polycystic kidney disease — inherited condition causing cysts
  • Recurrent kidney infections or urinary obstructions
  • Prolonged use of certain medications (NSAIDs, some antibiotics)
💡

Early CKD often has no symptoms. Many people don't feel sick until their kidneys are working at less than 15% of normal. That's why regular blood tests (eGFR) and urine tests (albumin) are so important — they catch problems early.

🔬 eGFR — Your Kidney Score

eGFR stands for estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate. It's a number that tells you how well your kidneys are filtering. Think of it like a percentage score:

  • 90+ = kidneys working normally
  • 60–89 = mildly reduced function
  • 30–59 = moderately reduced
  • 15–29 = severely reduced
  • Below 15 = kidney failure — dialysis or transplant may be needed

Your eGFR is calculated from a simple blood test (serum creatinine), adjusted for your age, sex, and body size. Ask your doctor for your latest eGFR number.

📊 The 5 Stages of CKD

CKD is classified into 5 stages based on your eGFR. Tap any stage to learn more.

1
Normal or High
eGFR ≥ 90
Kidney damage is present (protein in urine, structural abnormality) but filtration is still normal. Focus: identify cause, control blood pressure, manage diabetes if applicable. Most people feel completely well.
2
Mildly Decreased
eGFR 60–89
Mild loss of function. Usually no symptoms. Focus: lifestyle changes, blood pressure control, regular monitoring. Avoid nephrotoxic drugs (ask your doctor before taking NSAIDs or certain supplements).
3
Moderate
eGFR 30–59
Waste products begin to build up. You may notice fatigue, swelling in ankles, changes in urination, or mild nausea. Focus: nephrology referral, dietary adjustments (phosphorus, potassium), anaemia management.
4
Severe
eGFR 15–29
Significant loss of function. Symptoms may include persistent fatigue, poor appetite, nausea, itching, and difficulty concentrating. Focus: begin planning for dialysis or transplant, vascular access or catheter placement, patient education.
5
Kidney Failure
eGFR < 15
Kidneys can no longer sustain life on their own. Dialysis or kidney transplant is required. Symptoms: severe fatigue, breathlessness, nausea/vomiting, muscle cramps, itching, swelling. Peritoneal dialysis is one of the treatment options at this stage.

🏥 When Does Dialysis Become Necessary?

Dialysis is typically needed when your kidneys are functioning at less than 10–15% (Stage 5 CKD) and you have symptoms of kidney failure that affect your quality of life.

Signs That Dialysis May Be Approaching

  • Severe fatigue and weakness that doesn't improve with rest
  • Persistent nausea, vomiting, or loss of appetite
  • Fluid overload — swelling in legs, ankles, around eyes
  • Difficulty breathing, especially when lying flat
  • Dangerously high potassium levels (hyperkalaemia)
  • Metabolic acidosis that doesn't respond to medication
🎯

Early planning is key. Starting dialysis preparation (like catheter placement for PD) 3–6 months before you need it gives your body time to heal and you time to train. Ask your nephrologist about peritoneal dialysis — a home-based option that many patients prefer.
